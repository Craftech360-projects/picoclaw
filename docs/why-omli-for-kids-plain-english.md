# Why Omli's Listener Is the Right Choice for a Kids' Toy



---

## First, what are we choosing between?

The toy needs to know **when a child starts talking and when they stop**. That is a separate job from understanding *what* they said.

Think of it as the toy's **ears**. The ears decide "someone is speaking to me now" and "they have finished, it's my turn to answer."

We have two options for the ears:

- **Ten VAD** — what we use today. A general-purpose listener, built for people in general.
- **Omli** — a listener built specifically for children's voices.



---

## The 7 reasons to choose Omli

### 1. When it makes a mistake, it makes the *forgivable* one

Every listener gets it wrong sometimes. What matters is *how*.

- **Ten VAD's mistake:** it ignores very short sounds. In our tests it completely missed a child-length word — the toy would have heard nothing at all.
- **Omli's mistake:** it sometimes thinks a cough or a chair creak was speech.

If a child says *"yes"* and the toy just sits there, the child thinks the toy is broken. If the toy occasionally reacts to a noise, that's mildly annoying but the conversation continues.


### 2. Ten VAD was never designed for children

Children don't sound like adults. Their voices are much higher, much quieter, breathier, and full of pauses and half-finished words.

Ten VAD is a general tool. Omli's is built and trained specifically on children. That is their entire specialisation, and it's the hardest part of this problem.

### 3. Omli catches the beginning of the word; ours doesn't

Children often start a word softly and get louder — *"...hello?"*

Our current setup only starts recording once it is already sure someone is talking, so the soft beginning is lost. Omli keeps a short memory of the moment *just before* it was sure, so the whole word survives.

The start of a word is exactly what's needed to understand it.

### 4. Omli handles the hard part so we don't have to

"How long a pause means the child has finished?" is a genuinely difficult judgement, and kids pause a lot mid-thought.

With Omli, that judgement is their product and their responsibility, with proper settings we can adjust. With Ten VAD, we had to build that logic ourselves — and we found a flaw in our own version during this testing.




### 5. They've offered to put it inside the toy itself

Right now, sound travels over the internet to Omli's computers and back. That costs time, uses data, and means a bad connection can stop the toy hearing at all.

Omli has offered a version that runs **inside the toy**. That removes all three problems, and puts a child-tuned listener right next to the child's mouth — where being sensitive is an advantage instead of a liability.


---

## Being straight about the risks

This is a case for choosing Omli, not a claim that today's version is ready. Three things must be fixed first.

### The sensitivity dial doesn't work

Omli's listener is *very* sensitive — good for hearing children, bad in a normal room. There is meant to be a dial to adjust this.


Without it, we get the "hears children well" benefit *and* the "reacts to noise" cost, with no way to balance them. **This is the single most important thing for Omli to fix.**

### It's slow to notice the child has stopped

Omli takes between **1.3 and 4 seconds** to realise a child has finished talking. That delay is added to every single reply, on top of the time to think and speak.

Young children lose interest quickly. This is the difference between a toy that feels alive and one that feels broken.
